How v2 works

One product, start to finish — what you actually do, and how the knowledge graph grows underneath while you never once "manage a graph."

The mental model in thirty seconds

You work a loop of five moves — Explore → Shape → Decide → Build → Learn — with Check available at any moment. That's the whole public surface: one command, seven verbs. You can enter anywhere and revisit any move.

Underneath, everything you and the agent learn or decide lands as a typed record on one of five planes of product memory. You never name the planes; the verbs read and write them for you:

Three rules carry everything: every durable fact gets a typed ID and lives in markdown; records link to records (the links are the graph); and nothing generated or inferred becomes accepted truth without an adjudicated change. The old v0.1→v1.0 stages still exist — as an optional guided journey over these planes, not as the system itself.

The layer you touch

The planes are the engine room. You live one floor up, on surfaces — pages the system renders for you and, at decision points, hands you the pen. This is the layer the repo has been building toward for a year: first PRD.md as the interface, then the styled review pages, then the decision-point pages. In v2 it's first-class, with one rule underneath: every surface is derived from the planes and none of them is the truth — markdown stays canonical, and any surface can be deleted and regenerated.

Deliverables come in two species, one mechanism: Review pages arrive pre-filled with the agent's draft and ask for the few judgments only you can make (rank these risks, accept this architecture, choose the type); Intake pages arrive blank and walk you through authoring (a journey, a persona). Either way the page emits the exact record the graph expects, and adjudication closes the loop. Backtracking uses the same two faces: the as-of view shows the decision set as of any past moment (read), and a supersede deliverable retires a decision with a successor (write) — nothing is ever deleted.

The walkthrough: "Signal"

Say you have an idea: small SaaS teams get blindsided by churn. You want to build Signal, a churn-warning tool. Here's the whole arc.

Scene 1 — Day zero: init

"Set up Signal as a new product."
A deterministic script scaffolds the memory containers, writes the front-door summary, and reports state with typed directives (NO‑EVIDENCE, so the menu leads with explore). Nothing to read, nothing to learn — the graph is empty and the system says so.

Scene 2 — explore: the idea meets evidence

"I think small SaaS teams lose accounts because churn signals are invisible until the cancellation email."
The problem-framing playbook loads — alone, ~90 lines. It asks who exactly, digs for evidence, and runs the research recipes (1–3-star review mining on the incumbents, job-post salary math for "customer success ops"). Every observation lands on the Evidence plane with a tier and an upgrade condition: CFD-002 "lost a $14k account with zero warning — support thread" (tier 3: complaint with cost, confidence 3/5), CFD-003 "team exports usage CSVs every Friday as a manual ritual" (tier 2: workaround, 2/5, "→ 3/5 if five interviews confirm"). Speculation gets rejected at the door — tier 5 isn't storable. Later you run explore with the mom-test playbook; five interviews upgrade the tiers exactly as the records predicted they would.

Scene 3 — shape: evidence becomes intent

"Shape the commercial model — who is this for, what outcome, what would they pay?"
Shape playbooks convert validated pain into commitments, each linked back to the evidence that justifies it: BR-001 ICP "B2B SaaS, $10–50k MRR — not for enterprise" (informed-by CFD-004); KPI-001 "activation = first saved account within 14 days"; FEA-001…003 health score, alert, save-playbook — the P0 cut. Pricing won't lock yet: the WTP gate holds it open until tier 1–2 payment evidence exists. The value statement has to pass the landing-page-headline test before it's storable.

Scene 4 — decide: the first adjudication

"Decide the product type."
This is the system's defining move — and it happens on a Review deliverable, not in a chat scroll. The page assembles the candidate types side by side, each with its supporting evidence linked, the guardrails it would inherit, and the counter-arguments. You rank, pick "Slice — narrow wedge of the retention-suite category," add a line of rationale, and the page emits the record. CHG-001 lands on the Change plane recording what was decided, on what evidence, by whom, when — completing the page is the sign-off. The Slice guardrails become live policy: from now on check flags any pricing or channel decision that contradicts the type. Rejected alternatives stay queryable, so no future session re-litigates them.

Scene 5 — shape again: the experience and the contracts

"Design the journey from alert to saved account."
The experience-design pipeline runs as one continuous playbook family: persona → journey → screens → prototype. UJ-001 "alert → diagnose → outreach → saved" (designed-for PER-001), SCR-001…003 , then the technical contracts: API-001 health-score endpoint (enforces BR-002), DBT-001 accounts table. You review none of this as raw records: the journey renders as a journey-map view (trigger → steps → value moment), the screens as a flow, and the sign-off is an architecture-map deliverable you acknowledge. Coverage is mechanical: every P0 feature must appear in a journey, every journey on a screen — orphans force an add-or-cut decision now, not during build.

Scene 6 — build: code joins the graph

"Build the health-score service."
Build compiles a context pack (just the records this work touches — a few thousand tokens, not the repo), packages the work as a Change-plane unit, and writes tests first against the declared contracts. Code carries its address: // @implements BR-002. A deterministic pass extracts the code graph and bridges it to the spec records — so check can now answer build-vs-blueprint questions: contract-closure ✓, test-coverage ⚠ (one endpoint without a failing-path test — fixed before the unit closes). Anything ambiguous discovered mid-build lands as a proposal for you to adjudicate, never as a silent decision.

Scene 7 — ship, then learn: reality talks back

"We launched Tuesday. What's actually happening?"
Launch instrumented the Reality plane: MON-001 baseline (alert→save conversion), telemetry monitoring KPI-001. Learn compares reality against intent — activation is running at 19 days against the 14-day target. The drift view puts reality and intent on one chart — target line, actual curve, the gap named. That drift doesn't silently edit anything: it becomes evidence (CFD-012, tier 4 — real behavior) and a proposal (CHG-003 "expand the save-playbook library; hypothesis: playbook depth is the activation bottleneck") waiting on a Review deliverable. You decide, and the loop is closed — post-launch feedback flows through the same Evidence → Intent → Change circulation the original idea did.

Scene 8 — check, anytime

"Are we ready to raise prices?" · "What's stale?" · "Anything contradict the Slice guardrails?"
Check is the always-available consistency engine, and its answers arrive as views: the goal-scoped fitness view for whatever you're about to do (never one global grade), the conflict list, the freshness queue (" BR-003 unverified for 96 days"), coverage, drift. The front door keeps a standing summary of all of it — where things are, what's active, what needs you. The old numbered gates live here as an optional policy pack — if you want the guided v0.1→v1.0 walk, check can hold you to it; if you don't, the same conditions are still checked goal-by-goal. Deterministic scripts do the scoring; the model never grades its own homework.

What you never see

You never say "plane," never draw an edge, never assign an ID by hand. You talk about the product on the surfaces; the verbs file everything underneath. Two disciplines make that safe: proposals before acceptance (an agent can research, draft, and build, but only an adjudicated change touches accepted truth — and the adjudication surface is a deliverable page, not a buried chat reply), and evidence direction (memory is written from what happened — interviews, shipped code, telemetry — never from intentions).

Why the graph matters over time. At month one it's provenance — every commitment cites its evidence. At month six it's leverage — "why did we choose this?" answers itself, rejected options don't get re-argued, and a fresh session (or a fresh model) loads a few thousand tokens of exactly-relevant records instead of re-reading the repo. At month twelve it's the moat: the model is replaceable; the memory isn't.
